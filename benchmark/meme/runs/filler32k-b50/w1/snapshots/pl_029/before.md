Minimalist music is characterized by simple harmony, repetitive melodic patterns, slow harmonic change, steady pulse, and layered repetition and development. It emerged in the US during the 1960s-70s as a reaction against the European avant-garde's complexity and atonality, influenced by social movements and non-Western musical traditions. Key composers include Steve Reich, Philip Glass, Terry Riley, La Monte Young, and John Adams. Minimalism emphasizes simplicity, clarity, and repetition, contrasting with the European avant-garde's complexity, dissonance, and irregular rhythms. Initially, minimalist music lacked mainstream popularity due to its experimental nature, unconventional form, and repetitive patterns perceived as monotonous. Recognition grew in the 1980s-90s as minimalist music was integrated into film and television soundtracks. Despite skepticism about its repetitiveness and perceived simplicity, minimalist music's appeal lies in its emotional impact, accessibility, meditative qualities, and subtle structural and textural complexities such as phasing and harmonic variations. Its complexity and depth rely on precision, strict structural repetition, and unique textural effects, requiring focused attention to appreciate its nuanced musical architecture and emotional resonance.
